The sensor worked. Is the number right?

Two kinds of quality assurance that are easy to mix up, with worked examples from the Arbor Hills Landfill's own monitoring records.

When a monitoring report says the instrument was "functioning within specifications," it is answering one question: was the device running? It is not answering a second question: were the numbers it reported correct? A sensor can be powered, communicating, and free of fault codes while it reports a value that does not match what is actually in the air or the ground. The two questions need different checks, and passing the first tells you very little about the second.

The distinction in one line each. Hardware (and transport) QA checks the pipe: the device has power, its pump and flow are in range, it raised no fault code, the data logger downloaded, the file arrived. Data QA checks the payload: whether the values themselves are plausible, consistent with each other and with neighboring instruments, and traceable to a known reference. A third question sits beside both: whether the measurement point represents the thing you care about at all.

What each kind of check can and cannot catch

CheckWhat it confirmsWhat it cannot tell you
Power, communications, data download, "no fault conditions"The device ran and the data arrivedWhether the sensing element is responding to the gas
Pump flow rate within limitsAir was drawn through the instrumentWhether the reading of that air is correct
A file transfer or web request that succeedsThe bytes were deliveredWhether the bytes are right
Range and saturation checksA value is inside what the instrument can reportWhat the true value was when the instrument hit its ceiling
Flatline (zero-variance) checks and comparison with neighboring instrumentsWhether a channel is still responding to the worldWhich instrument is right, without a reference
Internal consistency (for example, gas percentages that should sum to about 100%)Whether a reading agrees with itselfWhether a self-consistent reading is accurate
Calibration or audit against a known reference gasWhether the sensor reads a known concentration correctlyAnything that happened between audits

The failure that hardware QA is blind to is a sensor that degrades or sticks while the device around it keeps working. Electrochemical gas sensors can drift or stop responding and still report a steady, plausible-looking value. Nothing in a power, flow or communications check looks at the value, so nothing in those checks changes. The examples below come from the public monitoring records for the Arbor Hills Landfill.

Worked examples from the Arbor Hills record

1. A fence-line channel that stopped moving

The landfill's perimeter network has six stations (MS-1 through MS-6), each reporting methane and hydrogen sulfide every hour to a public feed. For long stretches, several stations' hydrogen-sulfide channels reported a single constant value while the same station kept reporting and its methane channel kept varying. MS-5 read 0.0 ppb from May 28, 2025 (apart from one 0.1 ppb reading) and MS-2 read 0.0 ppb from June 30, 2025, both until mid-to-late September 2026. Other stations sat at a constant 3.0 to 3.2 ppb for months.

From the outside these stations looked like they were working: their data kept arriving, hour after hour. The problem showed up only in the values: a channel with zero variance for more than a year while its neighbors varied. That is a data-QA finding, made by looking at the numbers, not by looking at device status. The feed's own label did not help either: it marks readings below 7 ppb as "BDL" (below detection limit), apart from a few with no label, so a stuck channel and a working channel in clean air carry the same label. A sensor in clean air can legitimately read near zero for a long time, which is why the useful test is the combination of a flat channel, a varying sister channel at the same station, and varying neighbors.

Source: GFL's public perimeter feed (hosted by Barr Engineering), as documented in the perimeter air dataset's data-quality note, status as of October 9, 2026. The cause has not been publicly confirmed by GFL, EGLE or Barr Engineering.

2. A monthly report that answers the device question every month

The school monitor at Ridge Wood Elementary is operated by Barr Engineering, which posts a monthly report. The reports follow a standard set of numbered sections: equipment operation, data downloading, meteorological conditions, maintenance and audits. When something real happens, the reports say so, with dates: a failed pump in April 2022 ("A total of 27 hours of data were lost"), an instrument swapped for a spare in November 2023, a unit sent to the manufacturer for repair in July 2025. That channel is informative. When a report names a problem, take it at its word.

The reverse does not hold. In 65 of the 70 reports the equipment section says the monitor's "functioning and operations were within the manufacturer specified ranges" (often followed by "except as noted below"), which is a statement about the device. The audits section, the one place in the format where a check of the readings against a reference would be reported, reads "No audits of the network were conducted during the time period" in all 70 reports from December 2020 through September 2026. Five of the 70 reports mention the monitor units being sent out for routine or annual calibration (the last mention is November 2023), so calibration does happen; the reports after November 2023 do not mention it, and none reports a field audit. A month with no reported problem means no device problem was reported. It does not, by itself, show that the readings were checked against a known value that month.

Source: the 70 monthly reports posted at arborhillsmonitoring.com/Home/Ridgewood, read October 12, 2026.

3. A reading that disagrees with itself

Wellhead gas readings report methane, carbon dioxide, oxygen and a "balance" percentage, which together should come to about 100%. That gives a free internal-consistency check. In this project's wellfield dataset, 43 as-found readings in the operator's April–June 2024 report fail it (114 rows in the dataset, which also counts adjusted readings and rows the filing repeats); each misses 100% by 1.5 to 2.5 points. They are kept exactly as filed and flagged (validation_ok = no) rather than corrected or deleted, so anyone can see them. One of them is the half-year maximum: as filed, the hottest reading of the first half of 2024 was 154.4°F at well AHWW0279 on May 31, 2024, and that reading fails the gas-percentage check. Restricted to readings that pass, the maximum is 154.2°F (well AHWW312R, June 20, 2024). The difference is small, and a missed gas sum does not prove the temperature is wrong, but which number you report depends on whether you apply the check, so the choice should be stated.

Source: the wellfield gas and temperature dataset (release of October 9, 2026; method and validation rule in its README).

4. An instrument with a ceiling

Every instrument has a range. The operator's Wells of Interest reports record carbon monoxide in the wells with colorimetric (Dräger) tubes (from late 2023, monthly readings come from an "HCH meter," with "draeger tubes used as needed"), and the 2022–2024 tables state the tube scale: "5 - 700 ppm." A tube with that scale cannot report 14,000 ppm; at its ceiling it saturates. In April 2019, an EGLE air-quality inspector's report noted that a request for an alternate temperature operating standard from the landfill's then-owner, Advanced Disposal, included a 14,400 ppm carbon-monoxide reading at well AHW272R2 from January 2019 (the report does not say how it was measured), and that a more recent attempt to take a carbon-monoxide reading with a Dräger tube had failed: the tube "simply turned pink," which the inspector wrote possibly meant it was saturated or otherwise contaminated. A saturated reading is censored data: the true value is somewhere above the scale, and treating the ceiling as the value understates it. The same reports supplement the tubes with a quarterly laboratory analysis (ASTM Methods 3C/25), which is a reference-level check the tubes cannot provide on their own.

Sources: EGLE Air Quality Division staff activity report, April 2, 2019; the operator's HOV Wells of Interest status reports (2022–2024), Attachment 2. Both are in the public records.

5. A working sensor in the wrong place

Representativeness is a separate question from both device health and value accuracy. A thermometer can be accurate and still not measure what you need. Wellhead temperature is taken in the gas stream at the top of the well; the heat of concern is far below. In August 2019 an EGLE inspector watched a contractor lower a thermocouple down two wells. At well 290, the wellhead read 75°F; going down, the readings rose, with small dips, to 163.8°F at 160 feet (in liquid), and the July wellhead temperature on file had also been 75°F. Two weeks earlier, at well 259R3, the inspector recorded a wellhead temperature of 94.1°F with 9.5% oxygen, noted that a borehole reading taken in April 2019 had shown 175°F at depth, and wrote that air dilution meant the wellhead temperature was not representative for that well. Nothing in these readings required an instrument to fail: the wellhead number was measuring the top of the well.

Source: EGLE Air Quality Division staff activity reports, August 15 and August 28, 2019 (the landfill was then owned by Advanced Disposal; GFL took ownership on October 30, 2020). In the public records.

A short checklist for reading any monitoring record

  1. Separate the two claims. "The monitor was operating" and "the readings are correct" are different statements. Look for evidence of each.
  2. Trust affirmative disclosures. A report that names a failure, a lost-data window or a swapped unit is telling you something real.
  3. Do not read silence as verification. The absence of a reported problem is not evidence that the values were checked against a reference.
  4. Look at the numbers over time. A channel that does not move for weeks or months, while related channels and nearby instruments do, deserves a question.
  5. Know the range and the labels. Find the instrument's scale and detection limit. A value at the ceiling is censored; a "below detection" label can hide a stuck sensor.
  6. Use the checks the data gives you for free. Sums that should add to 100%, duplicate readings that should match, units that should agree.
  7. Ask for the reference check. Calibration and audit records against a known gas are the only routine way to confirm that a sensor reads a known value correctly.
  8. Ask what the measurement point represents. An accurate reading in the wrong place can still mislead.
